Twelve problems we are hired to diagnose

Clients typically describe the issue they can see: revenue down, margin gone, forecasts missed. Those are results, not root causes. Successful boutiques do not publish fifteen engines. They run one proprietary diagnostic system, then apply a different lens until the hidden constraint is proven.

How the system is used

You describe the constraint. We select the lens. The same diagnostic discipline — evidence, issue structure, quantified impact, prioritized roadmap — is applied. Scoring rules, models, and working programs are used in engagement, not displayed as a public toolkit.

One system. Distinct lenses.

A robotics plant that must raise throughput without buying another cell is not diagnosed the same way as a contractor losing market share, or a board asking whether a capital project still creates value. Those are different cuts of the same system: commercial, margin and capital, operations, supply chain, project controls, and leadership. Shared quantitative work — financial cases, uncertainty ranges, statistical process views — is activated only when the lens requires it.

1. Profit and cash turnaround

Typical hire: profits are falling, cash is tight, and every function has a different story. This lens separates structural margin from execution noise, sequences cash actions ahead of long programs, and produces a recovery roadmap leadership can own. It is not a generic cost-cut list.

2. Revenue engine and lost share

Typical hire: volume or win rate is slipping, pipeline looks full, and market share is leaking. We examine qualification, cycle time, pricing leakage, concentration, and whether delivery capacity can actually absorb won work. Fractional business development is the implementation path when the constraint is commercial leadership, not demand.

3. Margin, pricing, and cost-benefit

Typical hire: revenue is acceptable and profit is not. We isolate price, mix, discounting, and cost-to-serve, then test whether a proposed spend (people, equipment, software) creates value after cost of capital. Leadership sees a case with a range — not a single optimistic spreadsheet.

4. Throughput without adding machinery

Typical hire: an automation or fabrication plant is behind on orders and the proposed fix is another cell, another shift, or more overtime. The diagnostic finds the binding constraint, the hours lost to changeover, quality, and queue, and the contribution left on the table. Capex is a last resort after the constraint is exploited.

5. Supply chain, inventory, and working capital

Typical hire: warehouses are full, cash is trapped, and service is still unreliable. We look at days on hand versus fill rate, forecast quality, single-source exposure, lead time, and tariff-sensitive imports. The output is a path to release capital without starving customers.

6. Capital project and construction recovery

Typical hire: a live project is late, over cost, or both. This lens uses project-controls language — schedule integrity, cost performance, scope absorption, residual risk — and produces a recovery sequence, not a status meeting. It is a different cut from a P&L turnaround.

7. New market, new office, or new offer

Typical hire: leadership wants a second location or a new revenue stream. The question is whether the core engine can fund and operate the expansion. We test commercial maturity, operational stretch, and the cash case before headcount and lease commitments harden.

8. Leadership cadence and alignment

Typical hire: strategy is clear on paper and execution resets every week. This is a qualitative diagnostic with financial consequences: delayed decisions, conflicting priorities, and meetings that do not produce owners. We assess cadence, decision rights, and accountability, then attach the cost of delay where it is material.

9. Investment and capital allocation

Typical hire: a board or owner is being asked to fund equipment, software, or a new line. We structure the cost-benefit, test sensitivity to the few inputs that actually move the case, and show a distribution of outcomes when those inputs are uncertain. The model is used in the room with your numbers — it is not a public calculator.

10. Risk and uncertainty

Typical hire: the plan assumes a single future. We quantify the events that can break cost, schedule, supply, or revenue; estimate expected value; and separate noise from the few risks worth contingency. Risk is diagnosed as its own cut, then folded into the financial case and the roadmap — it is not a separate product you buy instead of operations work.

11. Quality, yield, and process control

Typical hire: scrap, rework, or customer escapes are eating margin, or a regulated process cannot demonstrate control. We separate common-cause from special-cause variation, locate the loss at the constraint, and size the profit of getting the process stable. Statistical procedures are used internally; the client receives the interpretation and the action list.

12. Diligence and the performance fact-base

Typical hire: an owner, lender, or buyer needs an independent view of operations, commercial quality, and value at stake. We apply the same system under a diligence clock: capability, constraint, cash, and risk — written for a board, not a training deck.

What you receive. What stays proprietary.

You receive a written verdict, a ranked set of actions, a financial picture with ranges where uncertainty matters, and a roadmap with owners. ISI retains the diagnostic engines, issue trees, scoring logic, and quantitative programs. Client data remains client data. That is how boutique firms advertise depth without handing a competitor the playbook.

Intake and discovery capture the facts we need to choose a lens. They are not the engine.

Name the constraint. We will name the lens.

A consultation is the right start when you are not sure which of these twelve is binding. Discovery follows when you are ready to put numbers on the table.
